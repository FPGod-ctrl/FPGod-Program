**What you currently hold**

Three policies, across two insurers:

1. **Income protection with TAL** — a benefit of **$10,000 per month** if you're unable to work, after a 90-day waiting period.
2. **Life cover with AIA** — **$100,000**, currently costing around **$300 a year**.
3. **Trauma cover with AIA** — **$100,000**, linked to the life policy above.

The trauma cover being *linked* to the life cover is the important detail, and it's the crux of the decision you're weighing up.

**The catch with dropping the trauma cover**

You asked what you'd save by cancelling the trauma component. The answer is less than you'd expect.

Because the two policies are linked, the pricing on the life cover currently benefits from sitting alongside the trauma. Strip the trauma out and the life cover gets re-rated on its own — my estimate on the call was that the life premium would jump from around $300 to somewhere in the order of **$500 to $600**.

So you'd be dropping $100,000 of trauma cover *and* paying materially more for the life cover that remains. That's a poor trade. I want to stress that the $500–$600 is an estimate off the top of my head rather than a quote — if you're seriously considering it, let me get the exact revised figure from AIA before you decide anything.

**My recommendation: hold the lot until you retire**

I know the premiums are frustrating. Paying for something you can't see or feel until the day you need it is genuinely hard to stomach, and I don't dismiss that at all.

But taking it in the round:

- **You're only one to two years from retirement.** This isn't an open-ended commitment — it's a defined, short runway. Over the grand scheme of what you've built, the cost between now and then isn't a bank-breaker.
- **You're still working, which means you still have income to protect.** If something happened in the next twelve months, the loss of income would be substantial, and there'd be no way to replace it. That exposure disappears the day you retire; until then, it's real.
- **The income protection premiums are tax deductible**, so the effective cost is lower than the figure leaving your account.
- **The pricing on your cover is still genuinely competitive.** $300 a year for $100,000 of life cover is a good deal by any measure.

The other thing worth saying plainly: once you do retire, the whole picture changes and much of this cover stops earning its keep. That's the natural point to review it properly and wind back what's no longer needed — rather than cancelling now, saving a modest amount, and carrying the risk through the final stretch of your working life. Same logic applies to Desmond's cover.

**On retirement — worth a proper conversation**

You asked about what retirement actually looks like, and I gave you some general thoughts on the call. I want to be upfront that it was exactly that — general — and doing it justice needs a longer sit-down where I can look at your actual numbers. But the broad shape of it:

**Superannuation and the family home remain the two best places for your money.** Despite all the noise and tinkering around super over the years, it's still streets ahead of any other structure available to you.

**From 60, once you've met a condition of release, income drawn from super is tax-free.** That's the whole game. To put a number on it: drawing **$80,000 a year from super is roughly equivalent to earning about $130,000 in a job** and paying tax on it. Same money in your pocket, $50,000 less of gross income needed to get there.

**The implication is that money sitting outside super is doing the same job while attracting tax it doesn't need to.** Given where you both are in the run-up to retirement, directing what you can into super over the next year or two is worth serious consideration — there are contribution caps and rules that need checking against your specific position, which is where a proper conversation comes in.
