While you're both working and on strong incomes, borrowing capacity is at its peak. Lenders assess on income, and that assessment gets harder the closer you get to retirement. If a larger facility is part of the plan, the time to arrange it is now, not in five years.

That larger facility is what makes **debt recycling** possible: borrowing to invest, where the interest becomes deductible because the borrowed funds are used to produce income. At a 6% loan rate the deduction reduces the effective cost meaningfully at your marginal rates, and the hurdle the portfolio needs to clear is correspondingly lower.

I do want to be straight with you: this is leverage. It magnifies losses as well as gains, and it only makes sense with a long time horizon and the capacity to hold through a downturn without being forced to sell. We'll model it properly rather than assume it.

**Estate planning — and the priority item**

**Please locate a copy of Jo's mother's will and check whether it includes a testamentary trust.** This matters more than anything else in this email, because it cannot be fixed afterwards.

Briefly, and I'll send fuller material: a basic will passes assets directly into the beneficiary's own name. A will incorporating a **testamentary trust** passes them into a trust the beneficiary controls and can use freely. Two consequences:

- **Tax.** Income can be streamed to children — and grandchildren are taxed at **adult marginal rates** rather than the penalty rates that normally apply to minors. On an inheritance of $700,000 to $1.25 million producing income each year, that difference compounds substantially. Held personally, that income simply lands on top of your already high marginal rate.
- **Asset protection.** Assets in the trust aren't held in your personal names, so they're insulated from a future relationship breakdown or creditor claim in a way that personally held assets are not.

The critical point: **a testamentary trust can only be created by the will itself.** If it isn't there, it cannot be added once someone has passed. If the will doesn't include one, that's a conversation to have with Jo's mother and her solicitor while there's still time.

You mentioned you don't fully understand these yet — that's completely normal, and I'll send through something readable rather than legal drafting.
