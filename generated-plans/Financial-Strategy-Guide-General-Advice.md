## Part A — Superannuation strategies

### Superannuation at a glance — key rates & thresholds (2025–26)

| Item | 2025–26 | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Superannuation Guarantee (SG) | **12%** of ordinary time earnings | From 1 July 2025 |
| Concessional (pre-tax) cap | **$30,000** p.a. | Rises to **$32,500** from 1 July 2026 |
| Non-concessional (after-tax) cap | **$120,000** p.a. | Rises to **$130,000** from 1 July 2026 |
| Bring-forward (3 years) | up to **$360,000** | Up to **$390,000** if triggered from 1 July 2026 |
| Carry-forward (catch-up) concessional | unused cap from up to **5 prior years** | Only if total super balance **< $500,000** at prior 30 June |
| Total super balance / general Transfer Balance Cap | **$2.0M** | Indexed; affects NCC eligibility & pension transfers |
| Preservation age | **60** | For everyone born on/after 1 July 1964 |
| Contributions tax (in the fund) | **15%** | **30%** (Div 293) on concessional contributions if income > $250,000 |
| Downsizer contribution | up to **$300,000** each, age **55+** | Home owned 10+ years; does **not** use the NCC cap |
| Death-benefits tax (taxed element to a non-dependant) | **15% + 2% Medicare = 17%** | **15%** (no Medicare) if paid via the estate |

*Co-contribution, spouse-offset and other income thresholds are indexed annually — confirm current-year figures before relying on them.*

### Strategy 1 — Maximise concessional (pre-tax) contributions

**The strategy.** Contribute up to the **$30,000** concessional cap each year via salary sacrifice and/or personal deductible contributions, instead of leaving that income to be taxed at your marginal rate.

**Who it may suit.** People earning above the 30% tax bracket with spare cash flow who want to build super and reduce income tax.

**How it works.** Concessional contributions are taxed at **15%** in the fund (30% if your income exceeds $250,000 — Division 293) rather than your marginal rate (up to 47% incl. Medicare). The **net benefit is the difference** — roughly **(your marginal rate − 15%)** on every dollar contributed. The cap includes employer SG, so available "headroom" is $30,000 minus expected SG.

**Things to consider.** Don't exceed the cap (excess is taxed and can incur charges); contributions are **preserved** until you meet a condition of release (generally age 60); for a personal deductible contribution you must lodge a **notice of intent to deduct** and have it acknowledged by the fund.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 Div 291; s290-170 (notice of intent).*

---

### Strategy 2 — Carry-forward (catch-up) concessional contributions

**The strategy.** If you haven't used your full concessional cap in recent years, "catch up" by contributing the **unused amounts from up to the previous five financial years** on top of the current cap.

**Who it may suit.** People with a **total super balance under $500,000** (at the prior 30 June) who have unused cap — often those who took career breaks, worked part-time, or have lumpy income (e.g. a bonus or a capital gain year).

**How it works.** Unused concessional cap accrues from 2018–19 onward and can be used for five years before it expires. In a high-income or capital-gain year, a large catch-up contribution can produce a significant tax deduction. Check your available unused cap on **myGov/ATO**.

**Things to consider.** The opportunity is **lost in any year your total super balance is $500,000 or more** at the prior 30 June — so it can be time-limited. Confirm your year-to-date employer contributions first to avoid exceeding the cap.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s291-20 (carry-forward).*

---

### Strategy 3 — Non-concessional contributions & the bring-forward rule

**The strategy.** Contribute after-tax money to super (up to **$120,000** p.a.), or **bring forward** up to three years' worth (up to **$360,000**) in a single year.

**Who it may suit.** People with money outside super (savings, an inheritance, sale proceeds) who want it in the lower-taxed super environment, and who are under the relevant balance and age limits.

**How it works.** Non-concessional contributions are **not taxed** going into the fund. The bring-forward amount depends on your **total super balance** at the prior 30 June and your age (you must be **under 75** on 1 July of the trigger year). **From 1 July 2026** the annual cap rises to **$130,000** and the 3-year bring-forward to **$390,000** — the amount is locked at the cap in the year you trigger it.

**Things to consider.** Your total super balance can reduce or remove your bring-forward room; exceeding the cap has tax consequences; money is preserved.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s292 (NCC cap & bring-forward).*

---

### Strategy 4 — Government co-contribution

**The strategy.** Make a small **after-tax** contribution to receive a matching contribution from the government.

**Who it may suit.** **Lower-income earners** (including part-time workers and lower-earning spouses) who can spare a modest after-tax amount.

**How it works.** If your income is below the lower threshold, the government contributes **$0.50 for every $1** you contribute, up to a maximum (commonly **$500**), phasing out to a higher income threshold. **Income thresholds index each year — confirm the current-year figures.**

**Things to consider.** You must meet work, age and total-super-balance tests; the contribution is preserved.

*Legislative basis: Superannuation (Government Co-contribution for Low Income Earners) Act 2003.*

---

### Strategy 5 — Spouse contributions & contribution splitting

**The strategy.** (a) Contribute to a **low-income spouse's** super to claim a tax offset; and/or (b) **split** up to 85% of your concessional contributions to your spouse's account to **equalise balances**.

**Who it may suit.** Couples with uneven super balances or incomes — useful for evening up balances ahead of the Transfer Balance Cap and for estate/tax planning.

**How it works.** A spouse contribution can attract a tax offset of **up to $540** where the receiving spouse's income is low (offset phases out as their income rises). Contribution **splitting** moves concessional contributions across to a spouse without using their NCC cap — helpful where one partner is much closer to a balance limit.

**Things to consider.** Eligibility and income thresholds apply; splitting is done after the financial year ends; both partners' caps and balances matter.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s290-230 (spouse offset); SIS Regs (contribution splitting).*

---

### Strategy 6 — Downsizer contributions

**The strategy.** From **age 55**, contribute up to **$300,000 each** (up to $600,000 per couple) from the proceeds of selling your **main residence** owned for **10+ years**.

**Who it may suit.** Older home-owners who are selling (or have sold) the family home and want to move capital into super.

**How it works.** Downsizer contributions **do not count towards the NCC cap**, have **no upper age limit** and **no work test** — so they can be made even when other contributions can't. They must be made within **90 days** of settlement.

**Things to consider.** Once in super the amount counts to your total super balance and Transfer Balance Cap; the home must have qualified (at least in part) for the main-residence CGT exemption; it can affect Age Pension means testing.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s292-102 (downsizer).*

---

### Strategy 7 — First Home Super Saver Scheme (FHSS)

**The strategy.** Save for a **first home** by making **voluntary** contributions to super, then releasing them (plus deemed earnings) for the deposit.

**Who it may suit.** First-home buyers (and adult children of clients) who can make voluntary contributions and want a tax-effective savings boost.

**How it works.** You can release eligible voluntary contributions — up to **$15,000 per year** and **$50,000 in total** — plus associated earnings. Concessional contributions used this way are taxed lightly, improving the effective savings rate versus a bank account.

**Things to consider.** Strict eligibility and release rules apply; only voluntary contributions count (not SG); apply to the ATO for a determination before signing a contract.

*Legislative basis: Taxation Administration Act 1953 Sch 1 Div 138 (FHSS).*

---

### Strategy 8 — Salary sacrifice vs personal deductible contributions

**The strategy.** Choose the most effective route to a concessional contribution — **salary sacrifice** (arranged through your employer, pre-tax) or a **personal deductible** contribution (made yourself, then claimed as a deduction).

**Who it may suit.** Anyone making concessional contributions — the better route depends on employer arrangements and cash-flow timing.

**How it works.** Both land in super taxed at 15% and count to the same cap. Salary sacrifice is automatic and smooth across the year; personal deductible contributions offer flexibility (e.g. a single contribution after a bonus or capital gain) but require a **notice of intent to deduct**.

**Things to consider.** Ensure salary-sacrifice arrangements don't reduce your SG base; keep within the cap counting **all** sources; lodge and have the deduction notice acknowledged before lodging your tax return.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 Div 291; s290-170 (notice of intent).*

---

### Strategy 9 — Consolidate & review fees, insurance and investment option

**The strategy.** Bring multiple super accounts together (where appropriate) and review the **fees, investment option and insurance** inside your fund.

**Who it may suit.** People with several accounts, paying duplicate fees/premiums, or in a default option that no longer fits.

**How it works.** Fewer accounts can mean lower total fees and less duplicate insurance. Reviewing the investment option ensures it matches your risk profile and timeframe.

**Things to consider — important.** **Do not close an account before checking the insurance inside it.** Insurance held in super can be **difficult or impossible to replace** if your health has changed — never cancel cover until any replacement is confirmed **in force**. Also check exit fees, in-specie/CGT impacts and lost benefits.

*Legislative basis: SIS Act 1993 (trustee duties); product PDS terms.*

---

### Strategy 10 — De-risk as you approach retirement

**The strategy.** Review your **investment option / asset allocation** as retirement nears, typically moving toward a more **balanced** mix to manage sequencing risk.

**Who it may suit.** People within ~5 years of drawing on their super, especially those heavily weighted to growth assets.

**How it works.** A market fall **just before** you start drawing income — "sequencing risk" — can be hard to recover from. Holding more defensive assets reduces the downside while keeping a growth engine for a long retirement. Switching investment options **within** a fund is generally **not a personal CGT event**.

**Things to consider.** Reducing growth slightly lowers expected long-term returns; markets still fluctuate; align the choice to your risk profile and timeframe.

*Legislative basis: SIS Act 1993 (investment strategy); fund PDS.*

---

### Strategy 11 — Transition to retirement (TTR) pension

**The strategy.** From **age 60**, start a **transition-to-retirement income stream** while still working — drawing a pension and (often) continuing to contribute.

**Who it may suit.** People aged 60+ who want to reduce work hours, supplement income, or run a tax-efficient contribute-and-draw strategy in the lead-up to retirement.

**How it works.** You can draw a pension (minimum 4% / maximum 10% of the balance each year) while continuing concessional contributions. On full retirement, the TTR converts to an account-based pension with tax-free earnings.

**Things to consider.** TTR earnings are not tax-free until you fully retire/meet a condition of release; rules and caps can change; pension capital remains market-exposed.

*Legislative basis: SIS Regs (TTR); ITAA 1997 s295-385 (ECPI on full retirement).*

---

### Strategy 12 — Retirement phase: account-based pensions & tax-free earnings

**The strategy.** On retirement, convert super to an **account-based pension** so that fund earnings — and the income you draw — are **tax-free**.

**Who it may suit.** People who have retired (or met a condition of release) and want a tax-effective, flexible retirement income.

**How it works.** Once in retirement phase, investment earnings on the supporting capital are exempt from tax, and pension payments are tax-free from age 60. Amounts moved into retirement phase are limited by your personal **Transfer Balance Cap** (general cap **$2.0M**, 2025–26).

**Things to consider.** Minimum drawdowns apply each year; amounts above the Transfer Balance Cap must stay in accumulation (taxed at 15% on earnings); capital remains market-exposed.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s295-385 (ECPI); Subdiv 294-B (Transfer Balance Cap).*

---

### Strategy 13 — Re-contribution strategy (lift the tax-free component)

**The strategy.** After age 60, **withdraw a lump sum** (tax-free at your age) and **re-contribute it as a non-concessional contribution**, increasing the **tax-free component** of your super.

**Who it may suit.** People whose super will ultimately pass to **adult, non-dependant children** and who want to reduce the death-benefits tax those beneficiaries would pay.

**How it works.** A non-concessional re-contribution is **100% tax-free component**. **Important (proportioning rule):** the **withdrawal** is drawn from your tax-free and taxable components **in the same proportion** they bear to your whole interest — it does **not** come only from the taxable component. So the strategy lifts the tax-free percentage gradually, often repeated over several years (and pairs well with contributing sale proceeds within the NCC caps).

**Things to consider.** Stay within the NCC cap ($120,000/yr or $360,000 bring-forward, rising 1 July 2026) and the total-super-balance and age (<75) limits; the benefit crystallises **on death** and depends on current law; funds become preserved again.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s307-125 (proportioning); s292 (NCC caps).*

---

### Strategy 14 — Death benefit nominations & minimising death-benefits tax

**The strategy.** Put **valid binding death benefit nominations** in place and structure who receives your super on death to manage tax.

**Who it may suit.** Everyone with super — particularly those with adult, non-dependant children.

**How it works.** Super **does not pass under your Will** unless directed to your estate. A death benefit paid to a **tax dependant** (e.g. a spouse) is **tax-free**. Paid to a **non-dependant** (e.g. an adult child), the **taxable component** is taxed at **15% + 2% Medicare (17%)** if paid directly from the fund, or **15%** if paid via the estate. Nominating spouse-first, then directing amounts for children through the estate/a testamentary trust, can reduce this.

**Things to consider.** Binding nominations must be valid and current (some lapse after 3 years unless non-lapsing); coordinate with your Will/estate plan; the re-contribution strategy (above) can reduce the taxable component in advance.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s302-145 (death benefits to non-dependants); SIS Regs (binding nominations).*

---

### Strategy 15 — High balances: Division 293 and the proposed Division 296

**The strategy.** Be aware of the extra taxes that apply to higher incomes and higher balances, and plan contributions accordingly.

**Who it may suit.** Higher-income earners (income over $250,000) and those with large super balances.

**How it works.** **Division 293** applies an **extra 15%** tax on concessional contributions for those whose combined income and contributions exceed **$250,000**. Separately, a proposed **Division 296** would apply additional tax on earnings attributable to total super balances **above $3 million** — **proposed, not yet law**; monitor before relying on it.

**Things to consider.** Even with Div 293, concessional contributions are often still tax-effective (30% in the fund vs up to 47% personally); the proposed Div 296 may change strategy for very large balances if legislated.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 Div 293; Division 296 (proposed).*

## Part B — Retirement income strategies

### The key risks a retirement income plan manages

| Risk | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Longevity risk** | Outliving your money — retirements can last 25–30+ years |
| **Sequencing risk** | A market fall **early** in retirement (while drawing income) is hard to recover from |
| **Inflation risk** | Rising costs erode purchasing power over a long retirement |
| **Tax & means-test risk** | Poor structure can cost tax or Age Pension entitlements unnecessarily |

---

### Strategy 1 — Set a clear income target and budget

**The strategy.** Define the **annual after-tax income** you want in retirement (essentials vs lifestyle), then work backward to the capital and drawdown needed.

**Who it may suit.** Everyone approaching or in retirement.

**How it works.** A target (e.g. a "comfortable" vs "modest" standard) anchors every other decision — how much to hold in super, how much to draw, and whether to top up with the Age Pension or part-time work.

**Things to consider.** Split spending into **essential** (cover with reliable/guaranteed income) and **discretionary** (fund from growth assets); revisit annually.

---

### Strategy 2 — Account-based pension (tax-free retirement phase)

**The strategy.** Convert super to an **account-based pension** so earnings and income are **tax-free**.

**Who it may suit.** People who have retired or met a condition of release.

**How it works.** Investment earnings on the supporting capital are tax-exempt and payments are tax-free from age 60. Amounts moved into retirement phase are limited by your personal **Transfer Balance Cap** (general cap **$2.0M**, 2025–26).

**Things to consider.** Minimum drawdowns apply each year (age-based, from 4%); amounts above the cap stay in accumulation (15% on earnings); capital remains market-exposed.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s295-385 (ECPI); Subdiv 294-B (Transfer Balance Cap).*

---

### Strategy 3 — Transition to retirement (TTR)

**The strategy.** From **age 60**, draw a TTR pension while still working to ease into retirement or run a contribute-and-draw strategy.

**Who it may suit.** People 60+ reducing hours or boosting super in the final working years.

**How it works.** Draw 4–10% p.a. while continuing concessional contributions; converts to a full account-based pension on retirement (when earnings become tax-free).

**Things to consider.** TTR earnings aren't tax-free until full retirement; rules can change.

*Legislative basis: SIS Regs (TTR); ITAA 1997 s295-385.*

---

### Strategy 4 — Manage sequencing risk with a cash/bucket approach

**The strategy.** Hold **1–3 years of income needs in cash/defensive assets** so you don't have to sell growth assets in a downturn.

**Who it may suit.** Anyone drawing an income from market-linked investments, especially early in retirement.

**How it works.** A "bucket" structure — cash for near-term spending, defensive for the medium term, growth for the long term — lets growth assets recover before you draw on them, softening sequencing risk.

**Things to consider.** Holding too much cash drags on long-term returns; rebalance the buckets periodically.

---

### Strategy 5 — Choose a sustainable drawdown rate

**The strategy.** Draw at a **sustainable rate** rather than just the legislated minimum or an arbitrary figure.

**Who it may suit.** Retirees balancing income now against not running out later.

**How it works.** Minimum drawdowns are age-based (4% under 65, scaling up). A sustainable rate depends on your balance, returns, longevity and Age Pension backstop — modelling helps set it.

**Things to consider.** Drawing too much risks longevity; too little may mean under-living your retirement; review yearly.

---

### Strategy 6 — Maximise Age Pension entitlements

**The strategy.** Structure assets and income so you receive any Age Pension you're entitled to (even a part pension brings valuable concessions).

**Who it may suit.** Retirees near the means-test thresholds.

**How it works.** Entitlement is the **lower** result of an **assets test** and an **income test**. Levers include drawing down assessable assets, timing, gifting within limits, home improvements (the home is generally exempt), and a younger spouse's super (super in accumulation for someone under Age Pension age is not assessed). **Thresholds and rates change each March and September — confirm current figures.**

**Things to consider.** Even a small part pension grants the Pensioner Concession Card; gifting and structuring have strict rules and deprivation provisions.

*Legislative basis: Social Security Act 1991 (means testing).*

---

### Strategy 7 — Keep working a little: the Work Bonus

**The strategy.** Earn some employment income in retirement without losing much (or any) Age Pension, using the **Work Bonus**.

**Who it may suit.** Age Pensioners who want to work part-time or casually.

**How it works.** The Work Bonus reduces the employment income counted under the income test, letting pensioners earn a buffer before their pension reduces. **Confirm current Work Bonus amounts.**

**Things to consider.** Applies to employment/gainful work income; interacts with the income test.

*Legislative basis: Social Security Act 1991 (Work Bonus).*

---

### Strategy 8 — Layer in guaranteed/longevity income

**The strategy.** Cover essential spending with **guaranteed or lifetime income** (e.g. a lifetime annuity) and fund lifestyle spending from market-linked assets.

**Who it may suit.** Retirees worried about longevity or market volatility, or who want certainty for essentials.

**How it works.** A lifetime income stream pays for life regardless of markets; certain lifetime products also receive **favourable Age Pension means-test treatment** (a portion of the purchase price and income is discounted).

**Things to consider.** Annuities reduce flexibility/liquidity and may have lower expected returns; weigh certainty against access to capital.

*Legislative basis: SIS Regs (income streams); Social Security Act 1991 (means-test treatment).*

---

### Strategy 9 — Tax efficiency in retirement

**The strategy.** Use the tax concessions available to retirees to minimise (often eliminate) tax on retirement income.

**Who it may suit.** All retirees.

**How it works.** Super pension income is **tax-free from age 60**; account-based pension earnings are tax-exempt; outside super, the **Seniors and Pensioners Tax Offset (SAPTO)**, the tax-free threshold and **franking credits** can reduce or refund tax. Order withdrawals to keep assessable income low.

**Things to consider.** SAPTO and offsets have income limits; franking refunds depend on policy settings; coordinate super and non-super income.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 (pension tax exemption); ITAA 1936 s160AAAA (SAPTO).*

---

### Strategy 10 — Manage the Transfer Balance Cap

**The strategy.** Plan how much moves into the tax-free retirement phase, and use both partners' caps.

**Who it may suit.** Couples and individuals with larger balances approaching the cap.

**How it works.** Each person has a personal Transfer Balance Cap (general **$2.0M**, 2025–26). Equalising balances between partners (via splitting/spouse contributions earlier) lets a couple shelter more in tax-free phase; excess stays in accumulation.

**Things to consider.** The cap is personal and indexed; exceeding it has tax consequences; plan before commencing pensions.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 Subdiv 294-B.*

---

### Strategy 11 — Reversionary pensions & estate continuity

**The strategy.** Set your pension to **revert to your spouse** automatically on death, for income continuity and tax efficiency.

**Who it may suit.** Couples wanting seamless income to the survivor.

**How it works.** A reversionary account-based pension continues to the surviving spouse (a tax dependant) — generally tax-free — and gives a 12-month window before it counts against their Transfer Balance Cap.

**Things to consider.** Coordinate with death benefit nominations and the estate plan; the survivor's own cap still applies after the grace period.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 Subdiv 294-B; SIS Regs.*

---

### Strategy 12 — Plan ahead for aged care costs

**The strategy.** Factor potential **aged-care fees** into the later-life plan rather than being caught out.

**Who it may suit.** Retirees and their families thinking about later life.

**How it works.** Home-care and residential-care costs (means-tested) can be significant; how the family home and assets are structured affects both fees and Age Pension. Early planning preserves choice.

**Things to consider.** Aged-care means testing is complex and rules are changing — seek specialist advice when the need arises.

*Legislative basis: Aged Care Act (fees & means testing).*

## Part C — Estate planning strategies

### An estate plan has moving parts

| Component | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Will** | Directs assets you own personally |
| **Superannuation** | Does **not** automatically pass under your Will — controlled by nominations |
| **Insurance** | Proceeds can go to a nominated beneficiary, the estate, or a policy owner |
| **Jointly owned assets** | Pass by survivorship, outside the Will |
| **Trusts / companies** | Controlled by succession of control, not the Will |
| **Powers of attorney** | Manage your affairs **while you are alive** but incapacitated |

---

### Strategy 1 — Have a valid, current Will

**The strategy.** Put in place (and keep updated) a valid Will that clearly distributes your personal estate.

**Who it may suit.** Everyone — especially after marriage, divorce, children, or buying property.

**How it works.** A Will appoints an executor and directs personal assets. Without one, intestacy laws decide — often not as you'd wish.

**Things to consider.** Review after major life events; note that marriage/divorce can revoke or alter a Will; super and jointly held assets usually fall **outside** it.

*Drafted by a solicitor.*

---

### Strategy 2 — Testamentary trusts

**The strategy.** Use a Will that creates a **testamentary trust** on death, rather than gifting assets outright.

**Who it may suit.** People with children (especially minors or grandchildren), blended families, or beneficiaries exposed to relationship/creditor risk.

**How it works.** Assets pass into a trust controlled by trustees you choose. Benefits: **asset protection** (e.g. on a beneficiary's divorce or bankruptcy) and **tax-effective income** — income distributed to minor beneficiaries from a testamentary trust is taxed at **adult marginal rates** (not penalty minor rates), which can be very tax-effective for grandchildren.

**Things to consider.** More complex and costly than a basic Will; benefits depend on correct drafting and trustee choice.

*Drafted by a solicitor.*

---

### Strategy 3 — Enduring Power of Attorney & guardianship

**The strategy.** Appoint **enduring powers of attorney** (financial) and a medical/guardianship decision-maker.

**Who it may suit.** Everyone — critical if illness or injury affects capacity.

**How it works.** An enduring financial attorney can manage money/assets if you lose capacity; a medical/guardianship appointment covers health and lifestyle decisions. These operate **while you are alive**.

**Things to consider.** Choose trustworthy attorneys; documents are state-based; especially important where a health diagnosis exists.

*Drafted by a solicitor.*

---

### Strategy 4 — Super death benefit nominations

**The strategy.** Put **valid binding death benefit nominations** in place so your super goes where you intend.

**Who it may suit.** Everyone with super.

**How it works.** Super **does not pass under your Will** unless directed to your estate. A **binding** nomination compels the trustee to pay as directed; a **non-lapsing** binding nomination doesn't expire after 3 years. You can nominate dependants directly, or your **legal personal representative** (estate) to flow through a testamentary trust.

**Things to consider.** Nominations must be valid and current; coordinate with the Will and the death-benefits-tax position (below).

*Legislative basis: SIS Act 1993 & Regs (death benefit nominations).*

---

### Strategy 5 — Minimise super death-benefits tax

**The strategy.** Reduce the tax adult, non-dependant children would pay on your super, in advance.

**Who it may suit.** People whose super will ultimately pass to adult children.

**How it works.** A death benefit to a **tax dependant** (e.g. spouse) is **tax-free**. To a **non-dependant**, the **taxable component** is taxed at **15% + 2% Medicare (17%)** if paid directly from the fund, or **15%** (no Medicare) if paid via the estate. Levers: nominate **spouse-first**; pay children's share **via the estate**; and use the **re-contribution strategy** (after 60, withdraw and re-contribute as a non-concessional contribution to lift the tax-free component — remembering the **proportioning rule** means a withdrawal is drawn proportionally from both components, not just the taxable one).

**Things to consider.** Re-contribution is subject to NCC caps, total-super-balance and age limits; benefits crystallise on death and depend on current law.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 s302-145 (death benefits to non-dependants); s307-125 (proportioning).*

---

### Strategy 6 — Reversionary pensions

**The strategy.** Make a pension **reversionary** to your spouse so income continues automatically on death.

**Who it may suit.** Couples drawing account-based pensions.

**How it works.** The pension continues to the surviving spouse (a tax dependant) — generally tax-free — with a 12-month window before it counts to their Transfer Balance Cap.

**Things to consider.** Coordinate with nominations and the estate plan; the survivor's own cap applies after the grace period.

*Legislative basis: ITAA 1997 Subdiv 294-B; SIS Regs.*

---

### Strategy 7 — Review ownership structures & jointly held assets

**The strategy.** Understand how each asset passes — many do **not** go through your Will.

**Who it may suit.** Anyone with joint property, trusts, or company interests.

**How it works.** Joint tenancy assets pass by **survivorship**; trust/company assets pass by **succession of control** (e.g. who becomes appointor/director), not under the Will. Aligning these with your intentions avoids surprises.

**Things to consider.** Tenants-in-common vs joint tenants has very different outcomes; trust deeds govern succession of control.

*Drafted/reviewed by a solicitor.*

---

### Strategy 8 — Coordinate insurance with the estate

**The strategy.** Make sure life insurance proceeds land where intended and tax-effectively.

**Who it may suit.** Anyone holding life/TPD cover, in or out of super.

**How it works.** Cover held **inside super** is paid as a death benefit subject to nomination and the death-benefits-tax rules; cover held **personally** can be paid to a nominated beneficiary or the estate. Structure ownership and beneficiaries to match the plan.

**Things to consider.** Insurance in super can be taxed to non-dependants; review beneficiary designations alongside the Will.

*Legislative basis: SIS Act 1993; ITAA 1997 (death benefits).*

---

### Strategy 9 — Business succession & buy-sell agreements

**The strategy.** For business owners, put a **buy-sell (succession) agreement** in place, usually funded by insurance.

**Who it may suit.** Owners of a business with co-owners/partners.

**How it works.** A buy-sell agreement sets how an owner's share transfers on death/disability, with insurance providing the funds — giving the family value and remaining owners control.

**Things to consider.** Requires legal and tax structuring (CGT, ownership of policies); review as the business changes.

*Drafted by a solicitor/accountant.*

---

### Strategy 10 — Blended families & equalisation

**The strategy.** Use trusts, life interests and beneficiary structuring to provide fairly across a blended family.

**Who it may suit.** Remarried couples, families with children from prior relationships.

**How it works.** Tools such as testamentary trusts, **life interests** (e.g. a partner can live in the home for life, with capital ultimately to children) and careful nominations balance competing needs and reduce the risk of disputes/claims.

**Things to consider.** Family-provision claims are a real risk — legal advice is essential.

*Drafted by a solicitor.*

---

### Strategy 11 — Gifting, advance equalisation & records

**The strategy.** Consider lifetime gifting/loans (often via a trust) and keep clear records.

**Who it may suit.** People wanting to help children now or simplify the estate.

**How it works.** A **documented loan** (e.g. from a family/testamentary trust) can help a child into housing while protecting the capital on a relationship breakdown, unlike an outright gift. Keeping an up-to-date asset register and document list eases administration.

**Things to consider.** Gifting can affect Age Pension means testing; document loans properly; review beneficiaries and records regularly.

## Part D — Personal insurance strategies

### The four main personal covers

| Cover | Pays out when… | Typically protects |
|---|---|---|
| **Life (death) cover** | You die (or are terminally ill) | Family, debts, dependants |
| **TPD** | You're totally & permanently disabled | Lump-sum needs if you can't work again |
| **Income protection (IP)** | Illness/injury stops you working | An ongoing % of your income |
| **Trauma / critical illness** | You suffer a defined serious condition | Lump sum for treatment/recovery |

---

### Strategy 1 — Start with a needs analysis

**The strategy.** Size cover from **need**, not a default — calculate the gap between what your family would require and the resources already available.

**Who it may suit.** Everyone, especially those with dependants, debt or a sole income.

**How it works.** A common framework is **debts + income replacement + future costs (education, etc.) − existing assets/cover**. The result sets the sum insured for each cover type.

**Things to consider.** Needs change with life stage; over-insuring wastes premiums, under-insuring leaves a gap; review regularly.

---

### Strategy 2 — Life (death) cover

**The strategy.** Hold enough life cover to clear debts and support dependants if you die.

**Who it may suit.** People with a mortgage, dependants or a single household income.

**How it works.** Pays a lump sum on death (or terminal illness). Can be held inside super (cash-flow friendly) or personally (more control, no preservation rules).

**Things to consider.** Need usually **falls over time** as debts reduce and assets grow; coordinate the beneficiary with your estate plan.

---

### Strategy 3 — Total & Permanent Disability (TPD)

**The strategy.** Cover the lump-sum cost of never being able to work again.

**Who it may suit.** Income earners, particularly with debt or dependants.

**How it works.** Pays a lump sum if you're totally and permanently disabled under the policy definition. Definitions matter: **"own occupation"** is broader (and usually can't be held in super) vs **"any occupation."**

**Things to consider.** Check the definition; TPD inside super may have a more restrictive definition and tax on payout in some cases.

---

### Strategy 4 — Income protection (IP)

**The strategy.** Replace a portion of your income (commonly up to ~70%) if illness or injury stops you working.

**Who it may suit.** Anyone who relies on their income — often the **most important** cover for working-age people.

**How it works.** Pays a monthly benefit after a **waiting period**, for a **benefit period** (e.g. 2 years, or to age 65). Premiums for IP are generally **tax-deductible** when held personally.

**Things to consider.** Longer waiting periods and shorter benefit periods cut premiums but increase your exposure; newer "income-protection" contracts differ from older "agreed value" ones — check terms.

*Tax note: personally held IP premiums are generally deductible; benefits are assessable income.*

---

### Strategy 5 — Trauma / critical illness

**The strategy.** Provide a lump sum on diagnosis of a serious condition (e.g. cancer, heart attack, stroke).

**Who it may suit.** People who want a buffer for treatment, recovery time or reducing debt during illness.

**How it works.** Pays on diagnosis of a defined condition, regardless of whether you can work. Generally **cannot** be held inside super.

**Things to consider.** Definitions and condition lists vary widely between insurers; premiums are not tax-deductible.

---

### Strategy 6 — Inside super vs outside super

**The strategy.** Choose where to hold each cover — inside superannuation, outside, or a mix.

**Who it may suit.** Everyone weighing cash flow against features and tax.

**How it works.** **Inside super:** premiums funded from your balance (easier cash flow), but reduces retirement savings, definitions can be narrower, and death cover paid to a non-dependant is taxed. **Outside super:** more comprehensive definitions and control, IP premiums deductible, but premiums paid from cash flow.

**Things to consider.** A common structure is **IP and some life/TPD in super** for cash flow, with **trauma and "own-occupation" TPD outside**. Beware default super cover lapsing on inactivity.

*Legislative basis: SIS Act 1993 (insurance in super); ITAA 1997 (deductibility & death-benefit tax).*

---

### Strategy 7 — Structure ownership correctly

**The strategy.** Get the **ownership and beneficiary** of each policy right for tax and estate outcomes.

**Who it may suit.** Couples, business owners, anyone coordinating insurance with their estate.

**How it works.** Options include self-ownership, super-fund ownership, cross-ownership (spouse owns the other's policy) and business structures. Ownership affects who receives proceeds, the tax treatment and whether proceeds form part of the estate.

**Things to consider.** TPD/trauma proceeds can have CGT implications depending on owner; align with the estate plan and any buy-sell agreement.

---

### Strategy 8 — Stepped vs level premiums

**The strategy.** Choose a premium structure suited to how long you'll hold the cover.

**Who it may suit.** Anyone taking out or reviewing cover.

**How it works.** **Stepped** premiums start lower but rise with age; **level** premiums start higher but rise more slowly — often cheaper over the long term if cover is kept for many years.

**Things to consider.** Level isn't "fixed" (it still changes with repricing); the breakeven depends on how long you keep the policy.

---

### Strategy 9 — Underwriting & pre-existing conditions

**The strategy.** Understand underwriting before changing anything — and **never cancel before replacement is in force**.

**Who it may suit.** Anyone reviewing, switching, or with a health change.

**How it works.** New cover is underwritten on your health; a new condition can mean exclusions, loadings or a decline. Existing in-force cover may therefore be **irreplaceable**.

**Things to consider — critical.** **Do not cancel or roll over existing cover until any replacement is confirmed accepted and in force.** Disclose accurately; check pre-existing-condition exclusions in default/super cover.

---

### Strategy 10 — Review on life events

**The strategy.** Re-check cover whenever life changes, not just at renewal.

**Who it may suit.** Everyone.

**How it works.** Marriage, children, a mortgage, a business, divorce, or rising assets all change the need — usually less life/TPD over time, but IP remains important while you're working.

**Things to consider.** Avoid duplicate cover across multiple super funds; cancel only once replacement (if needed) is confirmed.

---

### Strategy 11 — Beneficiaries & claims readiness

**The strategy.** Nominate beneficiaries correctly and keep policy details accessible for a smooth claim.

**Who it may suit.** All policyholders.

**How it works.** For super-held cover, a valid (preferably binding) nomination directs the death benefit; for personally held policies, the owner/beneficiary controls payment. Keep schedules, definitions and contacts on file.

**Things to consider.** Review nominations alongside the Will; understand each policy's definitions and waiting/benefit periods before you need to claim.

*Legislative basis: SIS Act 1993 & Regs (nominations).*

## Glossary

- **Concessional contribution** — a before-tax super contribution (employer SG, salary sacrifice, personal deductible); taxed 15% in the fund.
- **Non-concessional contribution (NCC)** — an after-tax super contribution; not taxed in the fund.
- **Carry-forward (catch-up)** — using unused concessional cap from the prior 5 years; available only if total super balance < $500,000 at the prior 30 June.
- **Bring-forward** — using up to 3 years of NCC cap in one year.
- **Proportioning rule** — a super withdrawal is drawn from the tax-free and taxable components in the same proportion as the whole interest.
- **Preservation age** — the age you can access super (60 for everyone born on/after 1 July 1964).
- **Transition to retirement (TTR)** — an income stream you can start at preservation age while still working.
- **Account-based pension** — a retirement-phase income stream; earnings tax-free within the Transfer Balance Cap.
- **Transfer Balance Cap (TBC)** — the lifetime limit ($2.0M, 2025–26) on what can move into tax-free retirement phase.
- **Taxable / tax-free component** — parts of a super balance; the taxable part can be taxed to non-dependant beneficiaries on death.
- **Tax dependant / non-dependant** — determines whether a super death benefit is tax-free or taxable.
- **Sequencing risk** — the risk of poor investment returns early in retirement while drawing income.
- **Means test** — the assets and income tests that determine Age Pension entitlement.
- **Testamentary trust** — a trust created by a Will, operating on death; provides asset protection and tax-effective income to beneficiaries.
- **Binding death benefit nomination** — a direction that compels the super trustee to pay your benefit as specified.
- **Enduring power of attorney** — authority to manage your affairs if you lose capacity.
- **TPD** — total and permanent disability cover; "own" vs "any" occupation definitions differ.
- **Income protection (IP)** — replaces part of your income during illness/injury; waiting and benefit periods apply.
- **Stepped / level premiums** — insurance premium structures that rise quickly with age vs more slowly.
